# K31.1 — Whose maintenance job

> **Category:** 2. KeplerOps · **Points:** 100 · **Difficulty:** medium
> **Solved by:** team agent

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Vulnerability / bug class** | Identity confusion in delegated scheduling — a maintenance job's *policy* decision and its *runtime* execution are evaluated under different principals |
| **Tools** | `kubectl`, `curl`, the platform's workload token exchange |

| **Flag** | accepted, value not recorded |

**What you are about to read:** how scheduled maintenance work carries several identities at
once, and how to prove which one a given operation actually checks.

## Objective

> Recover the scheduler's maintenance policy and establish which identity its different
> operations actually evaluate.

Two deliverables: the policy deciding *when and against what* maintenance may run, and an
identity map for its operations (read, evaluate policy, execute).

## Background you need

- A **schedule** is an object plus a controller. The object stores intent (cadence, target,
  channel/slot, window) and principal references; the controller materialises Jobs from it.
- Two authorization questions live here: (1) *may this caller see or alter the policy?* —
  answered by the API layer with the caller's identity; (2) *may this job touch the
  maintenance target?* — answered at execution time with the job's service account.
- When those answers come from different principals, a caller allowed to *describe* a
  schedule ends up holding its runtime authority — a **confused deputy**: the check is
  real, it is just asked about the wrong subject.
- Two faces: a platform object behind `cloud-api.keplerops.test` and the Kubernetes CronJob
  that actually runs, readable with the `fieldlink` kubeconfig.

## Walkthrough

### 1. Recon — enumerate the schedules, then the workload behind them

```bash
# from rowan-dev, through the SOCKS pivot
kubectl get cronjobs -n fieldlink -o wide
kubectl get cronjob <name> -n fieldlink -o yaml
curl -sS --socks5-hostname 127.0.0.1:1080 --cacert /home/kali/ctf/ca.crt \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $WORKSPACE_JWT" \
  https://cloud-api.keplerops.test/api/schedules/<schedule_id>
```

Read both side by side: the API view describes policy, the CronJob view describes execution.
Where they disagree about *who*, that disagreement is the finding.

### 2. Finding the flaw — three identities, one object

For the maintenance schedule, record four things: **creator** (annotation on the object),
**policy evaluator** (which credential the read succeeds with), **runtime identity**
(`spec.jobTemplate.spec.template.spec.serviceAccountName`) and **target** (env/args of the
template: channel, slot, target revision).

The card's phrasing — *"which identity its different operations actually evaluate"* — is the
hint that these answers differ. The policy read is scoped to your support principal; the
execution is scoped to the workload identity in the template.

### 3. Establishing the mapping instead of assuming it

1. Read the schedule with the ordinary developer/support credential: success means the read
   path evaluates *your* principal.
2. Read it with a credential that should not qualify (a token minted for another audience). A
   `403` proves the check is real and shows *which* principal is consulted.
3. Compare the template's runtime service account with the audience of your workload token. A
   match means the job runs with authority you already carry.

Solved by a team agent; the accepted value was not retained, so this records the technique
rather than a response body. The card scores the pair: policy plus identity mapping.

## Why this works (the vulnerability explained)

The bug class is **authorization at the wrong layer**: policy is attached to the schedule
object, but enforcement happens when the job runs, under a different subject.

- **Ambient authority.** A Job inherits the service account in its pod template, so whoever
  can write that field chooses the execution identity. Reading the object shows you that
  identity; it does not limit what you can do with it.
- **Split decisions.** The read/alter check and the run check use different credentials, so
  passing one says nothing about the other — and auditors who only read the API layer
  conclude the schedule is properly scoped.
- **Label drift.** Maintenance metadata ("this job is maintenance for slot X") is trusted
  data; if two services describe one object, the weakly validated copy defines reality.

The general lesson: for any scheduled work ask **three** questions — who may enqueue, who
decides the policy, who executes. If two differ, you have a deputy.

## Detection and fix

- **Detect:** schedules referencing a service account the creator's own workloads never use;
  schedule reads by principals absent from the job's audit trail; runs whose pod identity is
  broader than the policy they claim to enforce.
- **Fix:** bind the execution identity at creation to the authenticated creator and reject
  templates naming a service account the creator could not use directly. Re-check the
  maintenance policy (target, channel, slot) *inside* the job, not only in the API, and log
  the policy decision alongside the pod identity so the two correlate.
- **Fix the metadata split:** one source of truth for the maintenance label; the other view
  is a projection, not an independently editable copy.
